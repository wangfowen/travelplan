Things to do

Unique to Lisbon

  • Estufa Fria 4.7 ★ (12.6k) atlas obscura
    Botanical greenhouse built inside a disused 19th-century basalt quarry in Parque Eduardo VII; hundreds of plant species growing under slatted-wood shade structures. €3.70 admission.
  • Feira da Ladra 4.3 ★ (2.3k) my pick
    Flea market held every Tuesday and Saturday on Campo de Santa Clara, behind the National Pantheon — antiques, vintage finds, handmade goods.
  • Hospital de Bonecas 4.2 ★ (144) atlas obscura
    Family-run doll-repair operation founded 1830; a guided tour upstairs passes drawers of detached porcelain heads and limbs awaiting new bodies.
  • Mesa de Frades 4.1 ★ (1.2k) time out
    18th-century former chapel, walls floor-to-ceiling in azulejo tiles, converted into a fado house by musician Pedro de Castro; live fado nightly over dinner.
  • Galerias Romanas da Rua da Prata 4.5 ★ (177) my pick
    1st-century Roman cryptoporticus tunnels beneath the Baixa, rediscovered after the 1755 earthquake. Opens only a handful of days a year on a schedule set by the city — check lisboa.pt for this year's dates before planning around it; it may not be open during this window.

Museums

  • Calouste Gulbenkian Museum 4.7 ★ (17.8k) my pick
    Oil magnate Calouste Gulbenkian's personal collection, assembled while he lived in Lisbon during WWII: Egyptian antiquities, Islamic art, European painting, and a room of Lalique jewelry, set in its own gardens.
  • National Coach Museum 4.6 ★ (10.7k) my pick
    Around 70 ceremonial and traveling coaches used by European royal courts from the 16th to 19th centuries, one of the largest such collections anywhere.

Day trips

  • Sintra — Palácio Nacional da Pena 4.4 ★ (107k) and Quinta da Regaleira 4.7 ★ (66.8k) my pick
    Pena is a hilltop 19th-century Romanticist palace in candy-colored towers; Regaleira is an early-20th-century estate with a 9-story inverted "Initiation Well" and underground tunnels. €20 each, timed entry. Getting there: train from Rossio station, ~40 min, up to 6x/hour in summer; local Bus 434 connects the station to Pena. Both sites in one day is a full day.

Tours — walking and food, locally run

  • We Hate Tourism Tours — The Responsible Walking Tour 4.9 ★ (550) my pick
    Small groups (max 6), no megaphones or landmark checklists; flagship route runs through Bairro Alto and Graça's street art and former squats.
  • Taste of Lisboa — Lisbon Roots Food & Cultural Walk 5.0 ★ (1.1k) my pick
    Locally run food tour through off-the-beaten-path neighborhoods, tasting stops at markets and family-run kitchens along the way.

Events and festivals — during your dates

  • Jazz em Agosto — closing night Aug 9, 2026 visitlisboa.com
    The 42nd edition's final night of this jazz festival staged entirely on the grounds of the Calouste Gulbenkian Foundation: Chicago Underground Duo in the indoor auditorium at 18:30, then Anna Butterss's SML closes the festival in the open-air amphitheatre at 21:30. Food

Top picks

  • Cervejaria Ramiro 4.4 ★ (20.5k) michelin bib gourmand 2026 50 best discovery world of mouth culinary backstreets time out
    Art Nouveau three-floor seafood beer hall running since the 1950s. Percebes (goose barnacles), carabineiros (red prawns), prego do lombo (steak sandwich) to close.
  • As Bifanas do Afonso 4.5 ★ (6.5k) world of mouth
    Hole-in-the-wall pork-sandwich stand on Rua da Madalena, cash only, no seating. Sauce made with white wine and lard; €3.50 a sandwich.
  • O Velho Eurico 4.4 ★ (4.3k) world of mouth culinary backstreets time out
    Family-run tasca behind Rossio, chalkboard menu of regional dishes in a stone-walled room. Arroz de pato (duck rice), chicken hearts.
  • Belcanto 4.6 ★ (1.8k) michelin ★★ 2026 50 best #91 2025 público 3 garfos 2024
    Two-Michelin-star tasting menu of modern Portuguese classics from chef José Avillez, in an old Chiado townhouse.

Everyday institutions

  • A Ginjinha 4.6 ★ (3.6k) my pick
    Lisbon's original ginjinha bar, pouring the cherry liqueur since 1840 from a doorway near Rossio. Cash only, drink standing on the pavement.
  • Mercado de Campo de Ourique 4.4 ★ (2.6k) culinary backstreets
    Neighborhood food-hall market with a working fishmonger and produce ring where locals still shop mornings, quieter than Time Out Market.

Local dishes to try

  • Pastel de nata. Custard tart in flaky, laminated pastry. Where: Manteigaria 4.8 ★ (10.7k), Aloma 4.2 ★ (1k).
  • Bacalhau. Salt cod, Portugal's most-cooked ingredient. Where: A Casa do Bacalhau 4.6 ★ (4.2k).
  • Bifana. Soft roll stuffed with thin-sliced pork marinated in garlic and white wine. Where: As Bifanas do Afonso 4.5 ★ (6.5k) — see Top picks.
  • Percebes. Goose barnacles, hand-harvested off Atlantic rocks, steamed and eaten with your fingers. Where: Cervejaria Ramiro 4.4 ★ (20.5k) — see Top picks.

Where to stay

  • Campo de Ourique. Village-like residential grid on the Tram 28 line, not shaped by tourism. Day to day: its own market hall (Mercado de Campo de Ourique), everyday cafés, Jardim da Parada for a sit. Tradeoff: no metro station — Rato (yellow line) is a 15-minute walk, though buses run frequently.
  • Estrela. Quiet, tree-lined streets close to the center without the noise. Day to day: Jardim da Estrela, open since 1852, 7am–midnight — duck ponds, bandstand, genuinely used for running. Tradeoff: fewer restaurants right on the doorstep than the more central hill neighborhoods.
  • Alcântara. Former industrial district turned residential, flat where most of Lisbon isn't. Day to day: a riverside walking and running path toward Belém Tower (~7.5km one-way), Monsanto Forest Park nearby. Tradeoff: less historic streetscape than the hill neighborhoods — modern condo blocks rather than tile-fronted townhouses.

Avoid: Bairro Alto reads central and picturesque on a map, but it's Lisbon's main nightlife engine — 100+ small bars turn the streets into an open-air party every night, with noise and crowds until a 2am closing rule pushes everyone downhill to Cais do Sodré.

Practical

Tap water
Potable throughout the city, treated to EU standards — may taste faintly of chlorine.
Tipping
Not obligatory. Round up or leave 5–10% at nicer restaurants for exceptional service; €1–2 for a taxi; small change for hotel staff.
Cash & cards
Contactless works almost everywhere, including tapped directly at metro gates. Cash still matters at markets and small tascas — watch for handwritten "Só Dinheiro" (cash only) signs.

Getting around

Transit
Metro (4 lines), trams, Carris buses, 3 funiculars, and ferries across the Tagus — the network reaches residential neighborhoods, not just the sights. Pay by tapping a contactless bank card directly at the gates (tap the same card to exit) or with a reloadable Navegante card (€0.50). Single ride €1.90–1.92 (≈$2.05–2.10) · 24h pass €7.25 (≈$7.85) (2026). Metro runs 6:30am–1am; limited night buses fill the gap.
Rides
Uber and Bolt both operate widely (Free Now exited Portugal in 2023); Bolt runs 10–25% cheaper. Street-hail taxis are cream with a green roof, metered, with a 20% surcharge 9pm–6am, Sundays and holidays. Short cross-town €6–15 · airport–center official taxi €15–20 (≈$16–22), Uber/Bolt often €10–18 (2026). Known scam: touts inside the arrivals hall pre-negotiate inflated flat fares (reports of €80–120 for what should be a ~€20 ride) — use only the official rank, or book Uber/Bolt instead.
Verdict: rely on transit for cross-town trips, default to Uber/Bolt at night or with luggage — the metro stops at 1am. The historic center is walkable but hilly (Baixa + Chiado + Alfama in one day runs 6–7 hours with stops); Gira bike share (140 stations, e-bikes available) helps with the climbs, day pass ≈€2. Skip the Lisboa Card unless you're hitting 2–3 paid monuments a day — for a week based in a quiet neighborhood, a Navegante card with pay-as-you-go credit is the better value.

History in five beats

  • Age of Discoveries (15th–early 16th c.) Portuguese navigators pioneered European maritime exploration, building the first global sea empire — the monuments around Belém (Jerónimos Monastery, Belém Tower) date from this era.
  • The 1755 earthquake. A quake, fire, and tsunami leveled much of the city on Nov 1, killing 40,000–50,000; the Marquis of Pombal rebuilt downtown Baixa on a planned, earthquake-resistant grid — visibly different from the surviving hill neighborhoods like Alfama.
  • Estado Novo (1926–1974). António de Oliveira Salazar's authoritarian dictatorship, the longest in Western Europe.
  • The Carnation Revolution, April 25, 1974. A largely bloodless military coup ended the dictatorship and restored democracy; decolonization followed. The 25 de Abril Bridge is named for the date.
  • EU accession, 1986. Brought the economic modernization underpinning today's tourism and tech economy.

Culture

  • Fado. UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage since 2011 — a solo singer with a 12-string, pear-shaped Portuguese guitarra, originating in early-19th-century Alfama. You'll hear it in small neighborhood houses, not only staged tourist dinners.
  • Saudade. A word repeatedly described as untranslatable — a longing/nostalgia/absence tied historically to sailors' families who never knew if they'd return. Comes up constantly in conversation and fado lyrics alike.
  • Azulejos everywhere. Tiles cover churches, train stations, and ordinary house façades across the city, not just museums — introduced under Moorish rule, layered with Italian and Dutch influence, and used by the 17th century as pictorial "comic books" narrating history on public buildings.
  • Dinner starts late, Sundays go quiet. Restaurant dinner service usually doesn't start until 7:30–8pm; many restaurants close Sunday and Monday, and small independent shops shut Saturday afternoon through all of Sunday.
