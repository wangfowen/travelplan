Things to do

Top picks

  • Alcatraz 4.5 ★ (1.8k) my pick
    Federal penitentiary 1934–63, reachable only by the official NPS-concessionaire ferry from Pier 33 — no other operator lands there. The audio tour, narrated partly by former guards and inmates, runs the cellblock.
  • Sutro Baths & Lands End 4.7 ★ (3k) atlas obscura
    Concrete ruins of a seven-pool, glass-roofed 1896 bathhouse that burned in 1966, now open to the sea air. The trail continues to a working 19th-century Camera Obscura (a walk-in pinhole camera projecting the coastline live) and an unofficial stone labyrinth on the bluff above.
  • Musée Mécanique 4.7 ★ (8.4k) atlas obscura
    Free-entry arcade of coin-operated mechanical instruments and fortune tellers dating to the 1880s, still running on quarters, on Pier 45.
  • Cable cars my pick
    The world's last manually operated cable car system, in service since 1873 — grip men still work the cable by hand at each stop. Ride the Powell-Hyde line for the Hyde Street hill drop toward the bay.
  • Bernal Heights Park 4.8 ★ (159) the bold italic
    Free 360° summit at 475 ft, from the Golden Gate to San Bruno Mountain — less crowded than Twin Peaks and reached by a residential-street climb rather than a parking lot.

Unique to San Francisco

  • The Wave Organ 4.1 ★ (1.7k) atlas obscura
    Acoustic sculpture on a Marina jetty, built by the Exploratorium in 1986 from a demolished cemetery's granite and marble; 25 pipes turn wave action into low tones, most audible at high tide.
  • Wild parrots of Telegraph Hill my pick
    A naturalized flock of cherry-headed conures, descended from escaped pets, that has bred in the wild since the 1990s — the subject of its own documentary. Best odds on Greenwich or Filbert Street steps at dawn.
  • 16th Avenue Tiled Steps 4.8 ★ (2.1k) atlas obscura
    163 steps in Golden Gate Heights hand-tiled by neighbors in a community mosaic project, sea-to-stars themed, completed 2005.
  • Church of 8 Wheels 4.5 ★ (390) atlas obscura
    A deconsecrated 1906 church on Fillmore, independently run as a roller disco under the original stained glass and vaulted ceiling.

Views and nature

  • Twin Peaks my pick
    The classic 360° overlook at 922 ft — often above the fog line when the rest of the city is socked in. Free parking at the summit lot.
  • Salesforce Park 4.6 ★ (4.2k) my pick
    Free elevated park atop the Transit Center, reached by a free gondola at Mission & Fremont — 5.4 acres of gardens with downtown skyline views, open until 8 or 9pm.

Museums and galleries

  • SFMOMA 4.6 ★ (16.6k) my pick
    Matisse, Rivera, Warhol and a living wall across seven floors; Jacob Hashimoto's large-scale installation Giant Arc opens Aug 22, 2026, inside the travel window.
  • Exploratorium 4.7 ★ (8.6k) my pick
    Hands-on science and perception museum on Pier 15, founded by physicist Frank Oppenheimer in 1969 — built the Wave Organ above as a side project.
  • Minnesota Street Project 4.6 ★ (284) my pick
    Purpose-built Dogpatch complex housing 14 independent contemporary galleries (Jack Fischer, Anglim/Trimble among the anchors); free First Saturday walk each month.

Tours — walking and biking, locally run

  • San Francisco City Guides 4.9 ★ (2.4k) my pick
    Nonprofit founded 1978, ~300 trained volunteer guides, free (donation-based) neighborhood and history walks — Barbary Coast, Haight-Ashbury '60s, Victorian architecture. Book at sfcityguides.org.
  • Sidewalk Food Tours 5.0 ★ (761) my pick
    Independent SF-only operator running 3-hour small-group Chinatown and North Beach food-and-history walks with a fixed local guide roster. Book at sidewalkfoodtours.com.

Day trips

  • Muir Woods 4.8 ★ (20.6k) my pick
    Old-growth coast redwoods, some over 800 years old, in a fog-belt canyon. Advance parking or shuttle reservation is mandatory (book at gomuirwoods.com) — arrivals without one are turned away. Getting there: ~35–45 min drive across the Golden Gate Bridge; no direct transit.
  • Point Reyes National Seashore 4.8 ★ (7.7k) my pick
    71,000-acre peninsula of cliffs, tule elk, and a working 1870 lighthouse at the point — August is prime for whale-watching and elephant seal viewing. Getting there: ~1–1.25 hr drive; no transit.

Food

Local dishes to try

  • Mission-style burrito. Rice-free, foil-wrapped, invented in the Mission District in the 1960s–70s. Where: La Taqueria 4.5 ★ (7.4k).
  • Dungeness crab and cioppino. The tomato-and-wine seafood stew SF fishermen invented on the wharf; crab season runs Nov–June, so expect it frozen rather than fresh-caught in August. Where: Tadich Grill (est. 1849) 4.4 ★ (5.2k), Swan Oyster Depot 4.6 ★ (2.3k).
  • Sourdough bread. The city's naturally-leavened, tangy loaf, tied to a wild yeast strain first cultured here during the Gold Rush. Where: Tartine 4.5 ★ (5.9k), Boudin's original 1849 mother dough at Fisherman's Wharf.
  • Vietnamese-American garlic noodles. Butter, garlic, parmesan and fish sauce tossed with egg noodles — invented at Thanh Long in the 1970s and now a Bay Area staple nowhere else claims. Where: Thanh Long 4.3 ★ (1.7k).
  • Irish coffee. Whiskey, coffee, sugar and cream, brought to the US and popularized here in 1952. Where: The Buena Vista 4.3 ★ (5.5k), by Fisherman's Wharf.

Where to stay

  • Inner Sunset. Quiet and residential, bordering Golden Gate Park — the JFK Promenade car-free corridor and Stow Lake loop are steps away, giving it the best running access in the city. N-Judah streetcar runs straight downtown. Day to day: Whole Foods and local Asian markets on Irving St, several gyms nearby. Tradeoff: the fog belt — cooler and greyer than the rest of the city most mornings.
  • Noe Valley. Tree-lined and family-residential; 24th Street is cafés and boutiques rather than bars. Reached by the J-Church streetcar. Day to day: Real Foods and Whole Foods on 24th St, Dolores Park and the Douglass Playground stairs for running. Tradeoff: hillier terrain and less flat ground to run than a waterfront neighborhood.
  • Presidio Heights. Flat, tree-lined, low nightlife, borders the Presidio itself — forested trails leading to Crissy Field's waterfront path. Day to day: Cal-Mart and Bryan's groceries in Laurel Village, Fillmore St. cafés. Tradeoff: the most residential and least walkable-to-transit-hub of the three.
  • Avoid: Marina District and Cow Hollow. Both read as quiet on a map but Union and Chestnut Streets are genuine bar-hopping strips with sports-bar noise into the night — despite otherwise having the city's best waterfront running route at Crissy Field.

Practical

Tap water
Potable and unusually good for a major US city — sourced mainly from Sierra snowmelt via Hetch Hetchy.
Tipping
~20% is standard at full-service restaurants. Watch for a mandatory "service charge" (4–20%) already added to the bill at many SF restaurants — tied to the city's high minimum wage — which is separate from a tip; read the check before tipping again on top.
Cash & cards
Tap-to-pay works almost everywhere, including transit gates. A handful of cheap-eats institutions (Saigon Sandwich, Swan Oyster Depot, Good Luck Dim Sum) are cash-only.

Getting around

Transit
Muni (bus/streetcar/metro) covers the city; BART is a regional/commuter line reaching downtown and SFO but not a substitute for Muni within neighborhoods. Pay with a physical or app Clipper card, or tap a contactless bank card directly at Muni and BART gates — no setup needed. Muni single ride $2.85, day pass $5.70 (2026); BART to/from SFO ~$11.15 one-way. Cable cars are now $9–12 flat per ride, a novelty rather than practical transit. Owl network buses cover key corridors midnight–5am.
Rides
Uber and Lyft are both headquartered here and fully operational; Waymo's driverless robotaxi is now public and often near price parity with UberX (~$20 vs ~$17 average) with less surge. Short cross-town trip ~$10–20; SFO–downtown ~$30–50 (2026) — versus BART's $11.15 and often comparable speed. Rideshare pickup at SFO is Level 5 of the Domestic Garage, not curbside.
Verdict: transit for downtown-to-downtown trips, rides for anything crossing neighborhoods after dark or reaching the outer Sunset/Richmond quickly — Muni's flat network is fine for the sights but slow for lateral hops. The compact 7×7-mile core is walkable end to end in under two hours if the hills don't deter you; Bay Wheels bike share covers most neighborhoods.

San Francisco & the Bay Area

Tech scene

  • OpenAI — ChatGPT maker, anchored in Mission Bay (486,600 sq ft); reports 900M+ weekly users and roughly $13B 2025 revenue.
  • Anthropic — Claude maker, expanded its SF footprint to 650,000+ sq ft in early 2026; reports roughly $14B annualized revenue.
  • Databricks — data/AI infrastructure company, ranked among the leading SF-based AI companies in 2026 valuations.
  • Salesforce — the city's legacy tech anchor, headquartered at Salesforce Tower since 2018.

The AI industry's recent concentration in SF proper — rather than the Peninsula suburbs of the earlier dot-com and Web 2.0 waves — is visibly reshaping specific pockets (Mission Bay, Hayes Valley, SoMa), sometimes called the city's "fourth tech boom."

History in five beats

  • Gold Rush (1848–1850s). A small Mexican mission town exploded into a boomtown port almost overnight, cementing SF's identity as a magnet for outsiders and fortune-seekers.
  • 1906 earthquake and fire. Destroyed most of the city — the fire did more damage than the quake — and the rebuild produced much of the Victorian and Edwardian architecture visible today.
  • Beat and hippie counterculture (1950s–60s). North Beach's Beat poets gave way to Haight-Ashbury and the 1967 Summer of Love, fixing SF's identity as a countercultural capital.
  • Dot-com booms (late 1990s–2010s). Silicon Valley wealth concentrated increasingly in SF proper, driving the gentrification tensions still visible against the older bohemian city.
  • AI boom (2022–present). OpenAI, Anthropic and the broader generative-AI industry have re-centered the tech industry specifically in San Francisco, not just the wider Bay Area — real estate, talent and street-level culture reorganizing around it in real time.

Culture

  • "Karl the Fog" is a daily-life variable, not scenery. The marine layer creates sharp hyperlocal microclimates — the Sunset can be 55°F and socked in while Noe Valley is 72°F and sunny a few miles away, same afternoon. Fog-free neighborhoods carry a real housing premium tied to this.
  • The mandatory restaurant service charge. Many SF restaurants add a 4–20% "service charge" or "kitchen appreciation fee" to the bill, legally distinct from a tip and not guaranteed to reach the server — a direct product of the city's high minimum wage, and something a visitor used to voluntary tipping will directly notice on the check.
  • Steep hills as daily infrastructure. Running the Filbert or Lyon Street steps is a normalized local workout, not a tourist stunt — the terrain functions as built-in cardio for people who live here.
  • Extreme wealth and visible homelessness sit blocks apart. Because the city is a compact 7×7 miles, the contrast between the Tenderloin's street conditions and adjacent extreme wealth happens over a few minutes' walk in a way that surprises unprepared visitors more than in most large, sprawling cities.
