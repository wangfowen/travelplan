Top picks

  • Pearl Harbor — USS Arizona Memorial 4.8 ★ (27,830) · Pearl Harbor (west) nps
    An NPS boat takes you out to the memorial, which spans the sunken battleship.
    The program is free, but timed tickets on Recreation.gov carry a $1 fee each. They release 56 days ahead, plus a next-day release, at 3pm HST. There are no walk-up tickets, and standby is not guaranteed. Skyline's Makalapa station serves the site.
  • North Shore winter surf — Waimea Bay and ʻEhukai (Pipeline) 4.8 ★ (6,709) · North Shore both plans star-advertiser
    From October to March, north swells break on the reefs off Waimea Bay, Pipeline and Sunset, and you watch from the sand. The NWS issues an advisory at 15 ft and a warning at 25 ft. Watch from a beach park with a lifeguard tower; Waimea and ʻEhukai 4.8 ★ (1,247) both have one.
    Officials say do not swim or snorkel anywhere on the North Shore in winter, including Sharks Cove and Three Tables. Swells arrive suddenly after flat spells. The one road, Kamehameha Hwy, jams on big-swell days. Check hawaiibeachsafety.com and the NWS Honolulu surf forecast the night before (my pick).
  • ʻIolani Palace 4.6 ★ (6,106) · Downtown Honolulu atlas obscura ʻiolani palace
    The only royal palace on US soil. King Kalākaua had it electrified in 1882, and after the 1895 overthrow Queen Liliʻuokalani was imprisoned in an upstairs room. Take the docent-led tour (60–90 min) rather than the audio one.
    Tickets are sold online only, with no walk-ups and no same-day bookings. Sources disagree on which days run docent tours, so check when you book.
  • Hanauma Bay Nature Preserve 4.4 ★ (2,568) · Southeast (Hawaiʻi Kai) 小红书 star-advertiser
    Snorkeling inside a flooded volcanic crater, after a mandatory reef-education video. It faces southeast, so it stays snorkelable in December when the North Shore is closed to swimmers.
    Nonresidents $25 plus a 2.35% online fee, and $3 parking (2026, official). Reservations open at 7am HST two days ahead and sell out within minutes. Closed Mon and Tue. The lot can fill and turn cars away even with a reservation, and the water is clearest before 9am.

Unique to Oahu

  • Byōdō-In, Valley of the Temples 4.7 ★ (7,918) · Windward (Kāneʻohe) atlas obscura
    A concrete replica of Uji's Byōdō-in temple, set inside a Kāneʻohe cemetery.
  • Healer Stones of Kapaemahu 4.7 ★ (65) · Waikīkī atlas obscura
    Four boulders on Waikīkī Beach honoring four māhū healers from Tahiti, whom the story describes as dual male and female in spirit.
  • Ulupō Heiau 4.6 ★ (186) · Windward (Kailua) atlas obscura
    A terraced stone temple platform, centuries old, behind the Windward YMCA. It is still treated as sacred, so stay off the walls. It is a short stop on a Kailua or Lanikai day.
  • Hawaiʻi Daijingu Temple 4.4 ★ (122) · Honolulu atlas obscura
    A Shinto shrine that enshrines George Washington and Abraham Lincoln as kami.
  • Free lei-making and hula classes, Waikīkī · Waikīkī honolulu magazine
    Free morning classes, first come, first served. Waikiki Beach Walk Plaza runs hula from 9 to 10am and lei making with Outrigger. The Royal Hawaiian Center and Alohilani Resort run lei and intro-hula sessions too.
    December 2026 dates are not confirmed; check each venue's weekly calendar once you arrive.

Views and nature

  • Koko Crater Trail (Koko Head Stairs) 4.9 ★ (2,373) · Southeast (Hawaiʻi Kai) atlas obscura 小红书
    A WWII tramway line of 1,048 railway ties that climbs straight up the crater, with views over Hawaiʻi Kai and Hanauma Bay at the top. It is free, needs no reservation and has no shade, so go at dawn.
    The summit bunkers have been taped off since a fall in July 2025. You can't do the climb and Hanauma on the same morning.
  • Diamond Head State Monument 4.7 ★ (17,403) · Honolulu (Waikīkī edge) hawaii state parks 小红书
    A trail of about 1.6 mi round trip from the crater floor to the rim, past WWII bunkers, ending above Waikīkī and the city.
    Nonresidents pay $5 entry, plus $10 per vehicle if driving (2026, official). Reserve ahead at gostateparks.hawaii.gov, credit card only. Open 6am–6pm, last entry 4:30pm.
  • Mānoa Falls 4.7 ★ (5,480) · Mānoa Valley honolulu magazine
    A muddy rainforest trail of about 1.6 mi round trip to a 150-ft falls at the head of Mānoa Valley. Swimming at the falls is prohibited because of leptospirosis and rockfall, and the trail can flash-flood after heavy rain.
    The Paradise Park lot costs about $7 (2026, secondary); free street parking lower on Mānoa Rd adds about 1 mi each way.
  • Lanikai Pillbox (Kaiwa Ridge) · Windward (Kailua) honolulu magazine
    A steep 30–45 minute climb to WWII pillboxes above the two Mokulua islets, best at sunrise. It is slick after rain.
    Unconfirmed: blogs report that parking on the Lanikai loop has been banned since July 1, 2026. Plan to park in Kailua town and walk or bike in.

Food

Local dishes to try

  • Hawaiian food. Kālua pig (pit-roasted pork), laulau (pork steamed in taro and ti leaves), poi, lomi salmon, squid lūʻau and pipikaula (dried beef), ordered as a set plate. Where: Helena's Hawaiian Food (top pick, below), Highway Inn Kakaʻako 4.5 ★ (2,794) honolulu magazine — the laulau platter.
  • Poke. Raw fish (usually ʻahi) cubed and dressed with shoyu, sesame oil, limu seaweed and inamona (roasted kukui nut), sold by the pound at fish counters and supermarkets. Where: Ono Seafood and Off the Hook Poke Market (Healthy staples, below); Tamashiro Market honolulu magazine — a Kalihi fish market, the spicy ʻahi negi toro; Foodland honolulu magazine 小红书 — the supermarket chain's poke counter.
  • Plate lunch. Two scoops of rice, a scoop of mac salad and a protein (shoyu chicken, BBQ beef, chicken katsu); its offshoots are the loco moco (hamburger patty and fried egg on rice under brown gravy) and Spam musubi (grilled Spam pressed onto rice, wrapped in nori). Where: Rainbow Drive-In and Liliha Bakery's loco moco (Everyday institutions, below), Fukuya's okazuya case (top pick, below); Musubi Café Iyasume honolulu magazine — musubi with fried egg or Spam katsu.
  • Saimin. Hawaii's plantation-era noodle soup: thin wheat-and-egg noodles in a dashi broth, topped with kamaboko, char siu and green onion, often ordered with teriyaki beef sticks. Where: Shige's Saimin Stand (Everyday institutions, below); Palace Saimin 4.7 ★ (515) my pick — a Kalihi counter that serves only saimin, udon, won ton and beef sticks.
  • Malasadas. Portuguese yeast doughnuts brought by Madeiran plantation workers, fried to order, rolled in sugar and often filled with custard or haupia (coconut pudding). Where: Leonard's Bakery (Everyday institutions, below); Agnes' Portuguese Bakeshop truck honolulu magazine — Kailua, thin crisp malasada discs with a hole.

Where to stay

  • Waikīkī, Diamond Head end (Kapahulu / Kapiʻolani Park side). The legal hotel zone, and the only part of it that settles down at night. The quiet-end call is my pick and unverified. Day to day: ABC Stores for basics and Foodland for a full shop. You can run around Kapiʻolani Park, do the Kapahulu–Ala Wai loop or go round Diamond Head. Swimming at Waikīkī is safe in winter. The W Line bus runs straight from the airport. Kaimukī's Waiʻalae Ave food strip is about 1.5 mi up Kapahulu, with buses along the way. Tradeoff: it is still a resort strip. Hotel parking costs $30–55/night (2026). Rent a car only for the North Shore and windward days, and park it at the Waikiki Shell lot on Monsarrat Ave (~$6/day, Jan 2026). Avoid: rooms on Kalākaua or Kūhiō in mid-Waikīkī. They look central on the map but are loud late into the night.
  • Kakaʻako. A condo district of mid- and high-rises between downtown and Ala Moana, with Honolulu's highest Walk Score (94). Day to day: Whole Foods Queen is on site. You can run along Kakaʻako Waterfront Park or the Ala Moana Beach Park loop, and Ala Moana is also safe for swimming in winter. Buses run along Ala Moana Blvd and Kapiʻolani Blvd, and the W Line from the airport stops here. Tradeoff: legal places to stay are hard to find. Outside resort zones, Honolulu's short-term-rental minimum is 90 days (my pick, verify). Rail doesn't reach Kakaʻako until 2031. People sleeping rough are visible in the parks (my pick).
  • Kailua (windward side). A flat beach town over the Pali from Honolulu, and one of the island's most walkable. Day to day: Whole Foods, Target, Times and Foodland are in town. It's easy by bike. Kailua Beach is on the trade-wind side, so it usually stays swimmable in December, though Portuguese man-o'-war wash in on onshore days (my pick, verify). You can walk to the Lanikai Pillbox trailhead, where car parking is banned from Jul 2026 (blogs, unconfirmed). Tradeoff: Honolulu is ~30 min away by car or bus 67 (route number my pick, verify). Vacation-rental limits leave few legal places to stay. Plan to rent a car for the whole time you're based here.

Splitting: for Plan B's 6 nights, pick one town base. For Plan A's 9 nights, ~6 in Waikīkī or Kakaʻako plus 3 in Kailua is worth it if you would rent a car for those days anyway. In December the North Shore is a day trip rather than a base: winter surf closes its water to swimming and Kamehameha Hwy jams on big-swell days.

Practical

Tap water
Potable. Honolulu Board of Water Supply water is groundwater from the island's aquifers (own knowledge, not searched).
Tipping
Expected at sit-down restaurants, at about 18–20% of the pre-tax bill. Hawaiʻi's full-service average was 18.8% (Toast, Q2 2026). Large parties are often billed an 18–20% auto-gratuity, so check the bill before you add a tip. At bars, tip $1 a drink for beer or wine and about 20% on cocktails. Valet gets $2–5 (guides, 2026). The state minimum wage is $16/hr (from Jan 1, 2026).
Cash & cards
Cards and phone tap-to-pay work almost everywhere (own knowledge). Carry some cash for food trucks and farmers-market stalls (own knowledge) and for the bus if you have no HOLO card (exact change). Two places run the other way: Skyline rail takes no cash, and Diamond Head entry is card-only (DLNR, 2026).

Getting around

Sun Dec 13
Honolulu Marathon morning both plans: expect road closures through Waikīkī, around Diamond Head and along Kalanianaʻole Hwy until early afternoon. Avoid driving that morning (route closures: own knowledge; check honolulumarathon.org).
Transit
TheBus covers the island, including the city, Windward and the North Shore. Skyline rail runs from Kualakaʻi (East Kapolei) to Kahauiki (Kalihi) and stops short of downtown, Ala Moana and Waikīkī. For a visitor, it is useful only for the airport (Lelepaua) and Pearl Harbor (Makalapa). From the airport, the W Line bus runs to Downtown, Kakaʻako, Ala Moana and Waikīkī every 10–15 min (every 30 after 7pm). It allows one medium suitcase plus a carry-on (HNL airport). Pay with a HOLO card ($2), sold at Skyline station machines (including the airport) and Waikīkī ABC Stores. Tap it when you board the bus and at the Skyline gates in and out. The reader takes the first chip it sees, so keep the HOLO card away from your bank cards (HOLO). Whether a contactless bank card works on the readers could not be confirmed. Fares: a 2-hour pass is $3.00 (unlimited TheBus and Skyline), and daily charges stop at $7.50. The 3-day pass is $20 and the 7-day $45. Cash is $3.25 a boarding, with exact change and no transfers (thebus.org, effective Jul 1, 2026). Skyline runs 4am–10pm daily. Bus late-night service could not be confirmed.
Rides
Uber and Lyft both operate (Shaka Guide). Street-hailing isn't done here (own knowledge), and taxi supply has thinned. On Uber's own page, UberX between Diamond Head and the airport averaged about $32, about 11 mi and 23 min (Uber, 1-month average, Nov 2026). The airport-to-Waikīkī fare should be similar, about ten times the $3 bus. A short cross-town fare could not be confirmed.
Verdict: walk and bus in town, and rent a car for one or two days to reach the far coasts. Waikīkī is about 2 mi end to end, roughly 40 min on foot (own knowledge), and Kakaʻako is the city's most walkable district. Riding TheBus every day costs at most $7.50 a day, against about $32 for one airport Uber. Take the W Line in from the airport unless you have more than one big bag. For the North Shore and the Windward side (Kailua, Lanikai), a car beats both the slow bus and repeated $30-plus rides. Waikīkī hotels charge $30–55 a night to park, so rent only for those days. Biki bike share is $5 per 30 min, but about 800 bikes were missing in mid-2026 and many docks are empty (Civil Beat, Jun 2026). Treat it as a bonus if you find a bike, not a plan.

Hawaiʻi (the state)

GDP
$117.6B nominal total (BEA via FRED, 2024) · about $81k per capita (derived here: $117.6B ÷ 1,446,146 midyear population, 2024; no published nominal per-capita GDP figure was found). Per capita personal income was $69,520 in 2024, 96% of the US figure (DBEDT Data Book 2024).
Economy
Tourism, about 16.1% of GDP; federal government, about 11.1%; military, about 5.7% (all 2024, UHERO/DBEDT chart in Jan 2026 legislative testimony). Visitor spending was $21.75B in 2025 on 9.64M arrivals, down 0.6% from 2024 and still below the pre-pandemic peak (DBEDT via Garden Island, 2025). Agriculture is small: the last sugar plantation (Maui) closed in 2016, and seed corn was the top crop at about $114M in 2022, ahead of cattle and coffee. DBEDT's Dec 2025 forecast was +1.5% growth for 2026.
Languages
English and Hawaiian are both official state languages (own knowledge). English works everywhere. Hawaiian shows up on signs, place names and menus, and Pidgin (Hawaiʻi Creole English) is the everyday local speech.

History

  • Settlement and kingdom. Polynesian voyagers settled the islands; the date is contested, with AD 1000–1200 the best-supported range (Kirch's review of the archaeology). Kamehameha I, a chief from Hawaiʻi Island, united the islands as one kingdom by 1810, when Kauaʻi ceded.
  • Missionaries and sugar. American Protestant missionaries arrived in 1820, and sugar plantations followed in the 1820s–30s, importing Chinese, Japanese, Portuguese and Filipino labor. This is why the population and the food are as mixed as they are. In 1980 there were 14 sugar and 4 pineapple plantations on more than 300,000 acres; by 2017 both crops were under 5,000 acres.
  • Overthrow to statehood. On 17 January 1893 sugar and pineapple businessmen, backed by US troops, overthrew Queen Liliʻuokalani. The US annexed Hawaiʻi in 1898, made it a territory in 1900 and admitted it as the 50th state on 21 August 1959 (about 92–93% voted yes). Some Native Hawaiian groups contest the legality of the annexation.
  • Hawaiian Renaissance. From the 1970s, hula, the Hawaiian language and traditional voyaging were revived, and Kahoʻolawe, used as a bombing range, was returned to the state in 2003 after protests. The voyaging canoe Hōkūleʻa first sailed to Tahiti in 1976 (Hōkūleʻa date: own knowledge). Immersion preschools began in 1982.
  • Lahaina and now. The 8 August 2023 Maui wildfire killed 102 people (CBS reports 101), damaged or destroyed about 2,200 structures and displaced about 12,000. A $4.037B global settlement was announced in August 2024; payment status is not confirmed from a primary source. Construction began on the 60 Front Street homes on 3 October 2026, and a blessing marked the first commercial rebuild on Front Street on 13 October 2026. Tourism has not recovered to its pre-pandemic arrivals.

Culture

  • Kapu signs and heiau. A kapu sign means off-limits, and heiau (stone temple platforms) are living religious sites, not ruins. Where no boundary is marked, the outer edge of the structure is the limit; do not climb the stones, take anything, or stack rocks, since offerings (hoʻokupu) are left there. (DLNR Wahi Pana brochure)
  • Hawaiian on every sign. Place names are written with the ʻokina (ʻ) and kahakō (macron), and Hawaiian words are in daily English: mahalo, kōkua (help), and mauka/makai (toward the mountain/toward the sea) used as directions (own knowledge). The language was nearly lost; ʻAha Pūnana Leo immersion preschools began in 1982, and about 3,000 students were in 28 public immersion schools in 2023. Honolulu Magazine puts native-level speakers at roughly 10,000, under 1% of the state; other counts run higher depending on how speakers are defined. (National Geographic, Honolulu Magazine)
  • Pidgin. Hawaiʻi Creole English grew on the plantations from Hawaiian, English, Portuguese, Cantonese, Japanese and Filipino. You will hear it in shops and from drivers. The Census Bureau recognized it in 2015 but counted only about 1,600 speakers, while Ethnologue's estimate is about 600,000 first-language speakers (date not confirmed), so the numbers are unreliable. (NBC News)
