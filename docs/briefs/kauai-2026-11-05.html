Top picks

  • Nā Pali Coast by boat, from Port Allen 4.9 ★ (6,159) my pick
    A catamaran up the 17-mile roadless sea-cliff coast. Whale season peaks Dec–Mar, so a December trip is also a whale watch. Holo Holo Charters also runs a Niʻihau + Nā Pali snorkel trip.
    Winter swells run 5–15 ft. The captain picks the route about an hour before departure and may switch to the calmer side or offer a free reschedule. Some operators drop snorkeling from late-Nov–Apr afternoon trips. Book for early in your stay so a cancelled trip can be rebooked. No outfitters run Nā Pali sea-kayak trips in winter.
  • Hāʻena State Park: Kalalau Trail to Hanakāpīʻai Beach and Falls 4.7 ★ (1,164) atlas obscura
    The first 2 miles of the Nā Pali cliff trail lead to Hanakāpīʻai Beach. A valley spur continues inland to Hanakāpīʻai Falls, about 8 miles round trip from the trailhead. Kēʻē Beach is at the trailhead.
    Status: Open as of Nov 2, 2026 (reopened Oct 15), 7am–6:45pm daily. Kalalau beyond Hanakāpīʻai is conflicting: DLNR says permitted camping reopened Nov 2, but Kauai Now (Nov 5) reports the trail closed past mile 2. Check dlnr.hawaii.gov/dsp/important-park-notices before going.
    Entry, parking or shuttle must be booked on gohaena.com. Booking opens 30 days ahead at 12:00am HST: Nov 19 for Dec 19 Plan A, Nov 22 for Dec 22 Plan B. Parking sells out instantly; cancellations show up 7–8am. The shuttle from Waipā Park & Ride (Hanalei) is $40 round trip and is refunded if the service is suspended. Hanakāpīʻai stream floods fast in rain, and the beach is not swimmable. Kūhiō Hwy at Hanalei Bridge closed for flooding three times between Sept and Oct 2026, cutting off everything west of it.
  • Allerton Garden (NTBG), Lāwaʻi Valley 4.6 ★ (1,716) lonely planet
    A guided-only walk through the Allerton family's estate, laid out as outdoor rooms down to the sea, including the buttress-rooted Moreton Bay figs from Jurassic Park. Next door, McBryde Garden is self-guided and leaves from the same South Shore Visitor Center. Spouting Horn, a lava-tube blowhole atlas obscura, is a short drive down Lāwaʻi Rd.
    Status: NTBG is private and not under the DLNR closure, but its post-Lowell status is unconfirmed. Per NTBG, the visitor center is open Tue–Sat; Dec 24–25 hours are unconfirmed. Book at ntbg.org.
  • Kauaʻi interior by helicopter, from Līhuʻe my pick
    Overflies Waiʻaleʻale crater, Manawaiopuna ("Jurassic Park") Falls, Waimea Canyon and the Nā Pali valleys. With Kōkeʻe's trails and upper lookouts closed, this is the only way to see the interior this winter. Island Helicopters lands at Manawaiopuna; Jack Harter flies doors-off.
    Status: Post-Lowell operations and prices not confirmed. Rain cancels flights, so book early in your stay.

Unique to Kauaʻi

  • Hanapēpē: Swinging Bridge and Friday Art Night 4.3 ★ (2,270) atlas obscura my pick
    A plank suspension footbridge over the Hanapēpē River, still in daily use, in a one-street plantation town of galleries. On Friday evenings the galleries stay open and food trucks line the street.
    The only Friday in either window is Dec 25 both plans. Whether Art Night runs on Christmas is unconfirmed. The bridge is open any day.
  • Glass Beach, ʻEleʻele 3.4 ★ (927) atlas obscura
    A cove of tumbled sea glass washed out of an old dump. The lava beds at its south edge are the less-visited part. It is 5 min from the Port Allen boat harbor.

Views and nature

  • Waimea Canyon Lookout 4.9 ★ (4,467) my pick
    The main overlook into a canyon about 10 miles long and 3,000 ft deep, with red-and-green banded walls. It is about 40 min up Waimea Canyon Dr from Waimea town.
    Status: Only this lookout is open as of Nov 2, 2026 (reopened Oct 24), and fees are waived. Kōkeʻe Rd beyond it is closed, along with Puʻu Hinahina, Puʻu Ka Pele, the Kalalau and Puʻu o Kila lookouts, and every Kōkeʻe trail. Check dlnr.hawaii.gov/dsp/important-park-notices before going.
  • Kīlauea Point National Wildlife Refuge 4.7 ★ (1,248) my pick
    A lighthouse headland over nesting seabirds: mōlī (Laysan albatross, arriving from about November), red-footed boobies, and nēnē on the lawns. In season you can also spot whales from the point.
    Open Wed–Sat 10am–4pm, last entry 3:30. Tickets are $10 plus a $1 fee on recreation.gov, bookable 60 days ahead; Dec dates are already open. Wed–Sat days in the window: Dec 19 Plan A and Dec 23, 24 and 26 both plans. Dec 25 hours are unconfirmed. Lighthouse interior tours are suspended. Federal land, not under the DLNR closure, but post-Lowell status is unconfirmed.
  • Limahuli Garden & Preserve, Hāʻena 4.7 ★ (832) my pick
    An NTBG valley garden built around restored ancient loʻi kalo (taro terraces), with Makana peak above. It is self-guided by reservation and just short of the Hāʻena trailhead, so it pairs with the hike.
    Status: Not a state park, but its post-Lowell status is unknown, and it has closed after past floods. It sits west of Hanalei Bridge, which closes in floods. Book at ntbg.org.
  • Māhāʻulepū Heritage Trail, Poʻipū my pick
    A clifftop coastal walk east from Shipwreck Beach over lithified sand dunes and sea-cut limestone to the undeveloped Māhāʻulepū beaches, about 4 miles round trip. It crosses private land, not a state park, on the driest side of the island in December.

Food

Christmas Day falls in both plans: many family-run places (plate-lunch counters, saimin stands, small markets) close on Dec 25, and some keep short hours on Dec 24 and 26. Check that day's hours before you drive across the island. (Own knowledge.)

Local dishes to try

  • Saimin. my pick Thin wheat-and-egg noodles in a clear dashi-style broth with char siu, fishcake and green onion, the plantation-era noodle soup. Where: Hamura Saimin Stand 4.4 ★ (2,114) (East, Līhuʻe; see Top picks).
  • Hawaiian plate. my pick Kālua pig, laulau (pork steamed in taro leaf), lomi lomi salmon and poi, served as a plate. Where: Hanalei Taro & Juice Co. 4.6 ★ (167) (North, Hanalei), Kōloa Fish Market 4.6 ★ (1,562) (South, Kōloa). Both are in Top picks.
  • Poke. Cubed raw ahi dressed with shoyu, sesame oil, limu (seaweed) and onion, sold by the pound from deli counters. Where: Pono Market 4.7 ★ (1,067) (East, Kapaʻa; see Top picks), Foodland (Hawaiʻi's supermarket chain, with stores in Princeville and Kapaʻa) hawaiʻi magazine.
  • Musubi. my pick A block of rice topped with seared Spam (or another protein) and wrapped in nori, a convenience-store staple everywhere in Hawaiʻi. Where: The Musubi Truck 4.8 ★ (822) (East, Kapaʻa; see Everyday institutions).
  • Shave ice. Ice shaved as fine as snow, soaked in syrup and often finished with haupia (coconut) cream or a scoop of ice cream underneath. Where: Wailua Shave Ice 4.7 ★ (2,302) (East, Kapaʻa; see Creative), JoJo's Shave Ice (Waimea, Hanalei and Wailua; running since 1992, with house syrups and haupia topping) hawaiʻi magazine.

Top picks

  • Pono Market 4.7 ★ (1,067) hawaiʻi magazine East · Kapaʻa

Where to stay

You need a car wherever you base. Kauaʻi Bus has run a reduced, fare-free, every-two-hours schedule since Hurricane Lowell, and the 900/950 express routes were still suspended as of Nov 5, 2026.

  • Kapaʻa / Wailua (east). The island's one real working town, with a coastal path along the water. Wailua gets about 46 in of rain a year, between the dry south and the wet north. Day to day: Foodland and Safeway are in town. The Ke Ala Hele Makalae path is paved and runs along the sea from Kapaʻa to Kealia and Donkey Beach, for running or biking, and you can rent bikes in town. Kapaʻa is on the bus mainline. Tradeoff: Kūhiō Hwy slows to a crawl through Kapaʻa, and some roadside motels are noisy, so pick a place off the highway.
  • Poʻipū / Kōloa (south). The driest side in winter, at about 32 in of rain a year, though multi-day downpours still happen. It is a strip of condos and resorts, with Old Kōloa Town a few minutes inland for errands. Day to day: Big Save in Kōloa for groceries. The Māhāʻulepū Heritage Trail runs along the bluff past Shipwreck Beach and is the run. This is the one side where the sea is usually swimmable in December. Tradeoff: It feels like a resort more than a place people live, it takes a car to get anywhere, and the north shore is about 40 min away.
  • Hanalei — avoid in December. It is the most beautiful base, which is why it gets booked. But Hanalei gets about 85 in of rain a year, the winter surf makes its beaches unswimmable, and Kūhiō Hwy at the Hanalei Bridge closed for flooding on Sept 7, Oct 1 and Oct 31, 2026. Each closure cut off the town and everything west of it from the rest of the island. HDOT is also rebuilding the Wainiha River Bridge No. 3 abutment, further west. Princeville is on the near side of the bridge and has a Foodland, so it stays connected. It is just as wet, though, and it is a golf and condo development with a water-conservation notice in nearby ʻAnini. Visit the north shore on dry-forecast days.

Plan A Nine nights is enough to split the stay, about five in Poʻipū for the dry weather and the Port Allen winter boats, then four in Kapaʻa for town life and shorter drives to the early Hāʻena shuttle and Kīlauea. Plan B For six nights, stay in one place: Kapaʻa if the north shore matters most, Poʻipū if beach days do.

Christmas week is usually the island's most-booked week. That is our own knowledge, and availability for Dec 2026 was not checked, so book the base early.

Tap water
Drinkable from county mains (my knowledge). Caveat: the county issued a water-conservation notice for ʻAnini after the storm (as of Nov 5, 2026). Brown-water advisories after heavy rain apply to the ocean, not the tap. Check the DOH viewer before swimming near stream mouths.
Tipping
Expected. Sit-down restaurants: 20% is standard in resort areas, and the Hawaii average was 18.8% in Q2 2026 (Toast). Bars: $1 per beer or wine, about 20% on cocktails. Valet: $2–5 (2026 guides). Check the bill for an automatic 18–20% gratuity on large parties. State minimum wage is $16/hr (from Jan 1, 2026).
Cash & cards
Contactless cards work almost everywhere. Carry small bills for Sunshine Markets farmers' market stalls, roadside fruit stands and the bus, which gives no change (my knowledge; bus rule per county).

Getting around

Transit
One network, the Kauaʻi Bus. It is a commuter system along the coastal highway, running roughly Kekaha–Līhuʻe–Kapaʻa–Hanalei. It does not reach Hāʻena, Waimea Canyon or Kīlauea Point (route reach is my knowledge). The 900/950 express routes were not running (as of Nov 5, 2026). The post-storm fare waiver ended, and regular fares returned on Oct 1, 2026. Pay by exact cash (no change given), the Kauaʻi Bus Mobile Ticketing App or a Holomua smart card. Day passes are sold at Big Save and Times. I could not confirm whether the bus takes contactless bank cards. Single $2 · day pass $5 · monthly $50 (2026; county fare page, checked Nov 5, 2026). Could not confirm evening or Sunday hours. The one transit option that reaches a sight is the Hāʻena shuttle from Waipā Park & Ride in Hanalei. It runs every 20 min from 7:20am to 12:40pm and costs $40 round trip for ages 16+, park entry included (2026, gohaena.com).
Rides
Uber and Lyft operate and pick up at Līhuʻe Airport (LIH), and the local Holoholo app also runs. Drivers are scarce early in the morning and on the north and west shores, and there's no street-hailing. Uber's own estimates from LIH: Līhuʻe town $15 · Kapaʻa $27 · Poʻipū (Grand Hyatt) $29 (undated Uber page, seen Nov 2026 — could not confirm year). Could not confirm north-shore fares.
Verdict: rent a car at LIH. The $5 bus day pass is cheap but stops at Hanalei, misses Hāʻena, Waimea Canyon and Kīlauea Point, and is short its express routes. A single Uber leg costs $15–29 even on the busy east–south corridor, and you can't count on a ride back from the north or west shore. There is no walkable centre. Old Kapaʻa town and the paved Ke Ala Hele Makalae coastal path north of it are the only stretches that work on foot or on a rented bike. There's no bike-share scheme (my knowledge). If you stay on the north shore, use the Hāʻena shuttle rather than trying for the scarce parking slots.

Hawaiʻi (the state)

GDP
$117.6B nominal total (BEA via FRED, 2024) · about $81k per capita (derived here: $117.6B ÷ 1,446,146 midyear population, 2024; no published nominal per-capita GDP figure was found). Per capita personal income was $69,520 in 2024, 96% of the US figure (DBEDT Data Book 2024).
Economy
Tourism, about 16.1% of GDP; federal government, about 11.1%; military, about 5.7% (all 2024, UHERO/DBEDT chart in Jan 2026 legislative testimony). Visitor spending was $21.75B in 2025 on 9.64M arrivals, down 0.6% from 2024 and still below the pre-pandemic peak (DBEDT via Garden Island, 2025). Agriculture is small: the last sugar plantation (Maui) closed in 2016, and seed corn was the top crop at about $114M in 2022, ahead of cattle and coffee. DBEDT's Dec 2025 forecast was +1.5% growth for 2026.
Languages
English and Hawaiian are both official state languages (own knowledge). English works everywhere. Hawaiian shows up on signs, place names and menus, and Pidgin (Hawaiʻi Creole English) is the everyday local speech.

History

  • Settlement and kingdom. Polynesian voyagers settled the islands; the date is contested, with AD 1000–1200 the best-supported range (Kirch's review of the archaeology). Kamehameha I, a chief from Hawaiʻi Island, united the islands as one kingdom by 1810, when Kauaʻi ceded.
  • Missionaries and sugar. American Protestant missionaries arrived in 1820, and sugar plantations followed in the 1820s–30s, importing Chinese, Japanese, Portuguese and Filipino labor. This is why the population and the food are as mixed as they are. In 1980 there were 14 sugar and 4 pineapple plantations on more than 300,000 acres; by 2017 both crops were under 5,000 acres.
  • Overthrow to statehood. On 17 January 1893 sugar and pineapple businessmen, backed by US troops, overthrew Queen Liliʻuokalani. The US annexed Hawaiʻi in 1898, made it a territory in 1900 and admitted it as the 50th state on 21 August 1959 (about 92–93% voted yes). Some Native Hawaiian groups contest the legality of the annexation.
  • Hawaiian Renaissance. From the 1970s, hula, the Hawaiian language and traditional voyaging were revived, and Kahoʻolawe, used as a bombing range, was returned to the state in 2003 after protests. The voyaging canoe Hōkūleʻa first sailed to Tahiti in 1976 (Hōkūleʻa date: own knowledge). Immersion preschools began in 1982.
  • Lahaina and now. The 8 August 2023 Maui wildfire killed 102 people (CBS reports 101), damaged or destroyed about 2,200 structures and displaced about 12,000. A $4.037B global settlement was announced in August 2024; payment status is not confirmed from a primary source. Construction began on the 60 Front Street homes on 3 October 2026, and a blessing marked the first commercial rebuild on Front Street on 13 October 2026. Tourism has not recovered to its pre-pandemic arrivals.

Culture

  • Kapu signs and heiau. A kapu sign means off-limits, and heiau (stone temple platforms) are living religious sites, not ruins. Where no boundary is marked, the outer edge of the structure is the limit; do not climb the stones, take anything, or stack rocks, since offerings (hoʻokupu) are left there. (DLNR Wahi Pana brochure)
  • Hawaiian on every sign. Place names are written with the ʻokina (ʻ) and kahakō (macron), and Hawaiian words are in daily English: mahalo, kōkua (help), and mauka/makai (toward the mountain/toward the sea) used as directions (own knowledge). The language was nearly lost; ʻAha Pūnana Leo immersion preschools began in 1982, and about 3,000 students were in 28 public immersion schools in 2023. Honolulu Magazine puts native-level speakers at roughly 10,000, under 1% of the state; other counts run higher depending on how speakers are defined. (National Geographic, Honolulu Magazine)
  • Pidgin. Hawaiʻi Creole English grew on the plantations from Hawaiian, English, Portuguese, Cantonese, Japanese and Filipino. You will hear it in shops and from drivers. The Census Bureau recognized it in 2015 but counted only about 1,600 speakers, while Ethnologue's estimate is about 600,000 first-language speakers (date not confirmed), so the numbers are unreliable. (NBC News)
